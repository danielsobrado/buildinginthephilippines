## ok so you want SPC flooring for a beach villa

Daniel here. Been researching this for weeks because I'm putting SPC in a villa about 150 meters from the sea in Camotes Island, Cebu. And let me tell you, the more you learn about this stuff the more confusing it gets.

But here's what I figured out: SPC imported directly from China at around $6-8/sqm FOB lands in Camotes at roughly **440-550 pesos per sqm**. That's 40-65% less than what Wilcon charges for the same quality. The savings are real.

The catch? You need the right specs or you're throwing money away. And in our climate - 80%+ humidity, typhoon flooding, brutal sun - wrong specs means your floor is toast within a year.

So let me break down everything I learned. Fair warning, this got long.

## What even is SPC and why not just use tiles

SPC stands for Stone Plastic Composite. The "stone" part is actually calcium carbonate (limestone powder) - like 60-70% of the core is basically ground up rock. Mix that with 25-30% PVC resin, add stabilizers, heat it all to about 170°C under pressure, and you get this incredibly dense rigid material. We're talking 1,900-2,100 kg/m³ density. Heaviest vinyl flooring type there is.

Why does that matter for a coastal villa? Dimensional stability. The stone-heavy mix barely moves with temperature and humidity changes. Unlike...

**WPC (Wood Plastic Composite)** - has a foamed core with wood or bamboo flour mixed in. Softer underfoot which is nice, but the foam makes it less stable and more prone to denting. Heat makes it move around. Bad choice for a sun-exposed villa.

**LVT (Luxury Vinyl Tile)** - flexible vinyl, no rigid core at all. Needs perfect subfloor prep and buckles under heat. Just no.

And btw, "Rigid Core Vinyl" is just the umbrella term that covers both SPC and WPC. Marketing people use these terms interchangeably which doesn't help.

For Camotes? SPC wins over all other vinyl options. Only porcelain tile beats it in raw environmental resistance, but tiles need way more labor and prep to install. SPC clicks together like Lego.

### The 5 layers (this actually matters)

Every SPC plank is basically a sandwich. Top to bottom:

**UV coating** - super thin polyurethane layer. Good ones have 4-6 coats with ceramic beads or aluminum oxide mixed in for scratch hardness. This is what protects against fading in tropical sun. You want this to be good.

**Wear layer** - transparent PVC film, 0.1-0.7mm thick. This takes the beating from foot traffic, furniture, sandy feet etc. For the villa I'm going with **0.5mm minimum (that's 20 mil)**. Should last 15-25 years in a residential setting.

**Print/design layer** - high-res film that makes it look like wood. If you get EIR (Embossed in Register) the physical texture aligns with the printed grain and honestly you can barely tell it from real wood by touch. Adds 15-30% to production cost but looks incredible.

**SPC rigid core** - the structural backbone. 3.5-7mm thick. The click-lock profiles are milled directly into this layer. Quality depends on the calcium carbonate to PVC ratio, how fine the particles are (400-800 mesh), and what stabilizers they use.

**IXPE or EVA underpad** - 1.0-2.0mm for cushioning and sound. Here's a critical point for coastal: **IXPE is mandatory**. It has closed-cell structure so it's genuinely waterproof and won't grow mold. EVA has open cells that absorb moisture over time. In 80% humidity that's a disaster waiting to happen.

### The calcium carbonate ratio thing

ok so this is probably the most important quality indicator and nobody talks about it in plain language.

Sweet spot: **60-70% calcium carbonate, 25-30% PVC resin**, rest is stabilizers and processing aids.

Too much calcium carbonate (above 75%, PVC below 20%) = cheap filler territory. Core becomes brittle, chalky, crumbly. Click profiles snap during installation. Floor cracks under stress. You can see it in the cross-section, it looks like compressed chalk.

Too little calcium carbonate (below 55%, PVC above 35%) = acts more like flexible LVT. Loses the dimensional stability that makes SPC worth buying in the first place.

A typical quality factory formula uses roughly 75 parts PVC Type 5 resin to 225 parts heavy calcium carbonate (about 75% CaCO3 by weight), plus 6.5-8 parts calcium-zinc stabilizer and around 8 parts processing aids. If a supplier can rattle off these numbers without hesitating, good sign.

### Virgin vs recycled PVC - THIS IS HUGE

Pay attention to this one. It matters more than almost any other spec.

**Virgin PVC core**: light beige or cream colored, smooth consistent texture, minimal smell. Here's the trick - shine your phone flashlight against a cut edge. Virgin material is semi-translucent, light passes through.

**Recycled PVC core**: dark grey, greenish, or black. Feels dry and rough. Chemical/plastic smell. Completely opaque to light. Leaves powdery residue on your fingers when you touch the cut surface.

Why care? Recycled PVC has inconsistent composition leading to weak spots and uneven density. May contain unknown additives and heavy metals. Higher VOC emissions. Can't pass FloorScore or GREENGUARD certification.

Recycled material saves the factory 30-50% on raw material costs which is why budget products use it. And here's the scary part - some factories claim "100% virgin material" but substitute recycled in production. The flashlight test is your best friend. Always request third-party lab reports too.

### How thick should it be

For a coastal villa in the tropics: **5.0-5.5mm core thickness (6.0-7.0mm total with the IXPE pad)**. This is the sweet spot.

Why not thinner? 3.5-4mm cores save money but produce weaker click joints and can't bridge minor subfloor imperfections. Philippine concrete quality is... variable... so you want that extra thickness tolerance. 4mm is maybe acceptable for bedrooms only.

One thing that surprised me - there's a proportionality issue. An overly thick wear layer on a thin core (like 0.7mm wear on 3.5mm core) actually increases cupping risk because the PVC wear layer and the mineral-heavy core expand at different rates. Keep it balanced.

### Click-lock systems and why you should care about patents

Three companies own the click-lock patents worldwide:

- **Unilin (Uniclic)** - owned by Mohawk Industries in Belgium. Industry benchmark. Angle-and-tap installation.
- **Valinge** - Swedish company. Their 5Gi push-down system was designed specifically for rigid core products.
- **I4F** - Netherlands based. Market share leader in SPC through flexible licensing terms.

Licensed systems add roughly $0.10-0.20/sqm to the product cost. That's nothing. But they deliver better joint strength, tighter tolerances, and way fewer installation failures.

Generic Chinese click systems skip the license fee and produce inconsistent milling tolerances. Gaps, separation, installation failures.

Here's something most people don't know: the **L2C (License to Click)** label program requires colored labels on every box of licensed flooring. No labels = unlicensed, regardless of what the factory claims. Look for them.

Philippines doesn't really enforce click patents compared to US or EU, but the quality difference between licensed and generic is real and worth the extra cents.

## How to actually test SPC quality

### Lab tests to demand from suppliers

Ask for test reports on these specific parameters. If a supplier can't provide them... find another supplier:

- **Dimensional stability** (EN 434 / ASTM F2199): change ≤0.1%, premium is ≤0.05%
- **Curling**: ≤1.0mm at 80°C for 6 hours
- **Residual indentation** (EN ISO 24343-1): ≤0.10mm recovery
- **Click tension strength**: ≥150 kgf/5cm
- **Wear layer peeling strength** (EN 431): ≥5.3 kgf/5cm - this predicts delamination
- **Abrasion resistance** (EN 660-2): Group T classification
- **Light fastness** (EN ISO 105-B02): **Grade 7+ on the blue wool scale** - this is critical for tropical sun. Standard minimum is Grade 6 but 150m from the sea with UV reflecting off water? you need 7+
- **Formaldehyde**: E0 class (≤0.07 ppm) or non-detectable
- **Slip resistance**: coefficient ≥0.8 dry, ≥0.6 wet

### Which certifications aren't BS

**FloorScore** (SCS Global Services) - this one's legit. Tests 35 individual VOCs, requires factory audits. BUT a 2022 investigation found about 10% of online SPC sellers using forged FloorScore logos. Always verify on the SCS Green Products Guide database. Don't take their word for it.

**CE marking** (EN 14041) - mandatory for European sales. Useful as proof of safety testing.

**GREENGUARD Gold** - more stringent than standard GREENGUARD. Tests for phthalates.

**SGS and Intertek reports** - careful here. These are testing labs, not certifications. An SGS report only validates specific parameters on specific samples at a specific time. A generic "SGS tested" claim without specifying which tests, which products, what dates? meaningless marketing.

### DIY tests before you commit to a big order

When your samples arrive, do these before ordering a container:

**Boiling water test**: submerge a sample for 2-3 hours. Quality SPC shows zero warping, delamination, or shape change. Seriously, nothing should happen.

**Bend test**: flex the plank firmly. Virgin SPC is rigid and resists bending without cracking. Too flexible = excess PVC or recycled content. Click profiles snap = too much CaCO3, too brittle.

**Smell test**: virgin SPC has barely any odor. Persistent chemical or plastic smell = recycled PVC.

**Density test**: weigh a sample, calculate density. A 150mm x 150mm x 5mm piece should weigh roughly 225g, giving about 2,000 kg/m³. Target 1,900-2,100. Below 1,800 is substandard. Above 2,200 could mean excess brittle filler.

**Flashlight test**: shine your phone light against the core edge. Virgin = some light passes through. Recycled = completely opaque.

**Joint water test**: click two planks together, drip water on the seam, wait 3-5 hours, separate, check for water marks in the click profile.

If it fails any of these, don't order. doesn't matter how good the price is.

## Living 150 meters from the sea with SPC floors

### Humidity - the good news and the caveats

SPC's core has no organic material. It doesn't absorb moisture from the air. This makes it fundamentally better than laminate (moisture-absorbing HDF core), engineered hardwood (cups and swells), and WPC (less stable). I found a documented Florida coastal installation showing no gaps after two years where hardwood had warped in identical conditions.

But. Manufacturer specs assume 30-55% indoor humidity. **Camotes is 80%+ constantly.** Way outside spec.

The SPC core itself survives. But three things can go wrong:

1. Moisture vapor from uncured or unbarriered concrete creates problems underneath the floor
2. Organic debris (dust, dirt, adhesive residue) trapped between SPC and subfloor becomes mold food
3. Adhesive bonds in glue-down installations degrade in sustained humidity

Found a documented case from Malaysia where SPC warped in a tropical condo within months. Sounds scary right? But the root cause was an uneven subfloor plus 15% moisture readings and double foam layers. Installation fail, not material fail. So... install it right.

### Sunlight is actually the bigger threat

SPC works between **13°C and 38°C** ambient. Maximum tolerance is around 65°C before deformation risk goes way up.

Here's the math that matters. Thermal expansion is about 0.1-0.2% per 10°C change. In a room with a 10-meter run where floor surface temp goes from 25°C to 50°C under direct tropical sun through windows (totally realistic in Camotes), the floor expands by roughly **2.5-5mm linearly**.

That's manageable with proper expansion gaps. But it absolutely requires planning.

A Philippine supplier called Decocity specifically warns that SPC in direct sunlight areas can hit 60°C. Getting close to the limit. And UV exposure is intensified about 25% in coastal locations from water and sand reflection.

Non-negotiable mitigations:
- UV-protective window film on all sea-facing glass
- Wide roof overhangs (standard Filipino architecture, thankfully)
- Curtains or blinds during peak sun
- Grade 7+ light fastness products
- **10-12mm expansion gaps** not the standard 6-8mm

### Wait is SPC actually waterproof?

Yes and no. This drives me crazy how it's marketed.

The individual plank? 100% waterproof. The limestone-PVC matrix won't absorb water even after 96 hours submerged in lab testing. Won't swell, won't delaminate.

But **the installed floor system is NOT a waterproof barrier**. Water seeps through click-lock joints when it sits on the surface long enough. Once underneath, it's trapped between the waterproof SPC above and the subfloor below. Hello mold.

For typhoon flooding on Camotes (real risk): planks survive submersion but locking mechanisms get damaged during removal so you basically can't reuse them. No manufacturer warranty covers flood damage obviously.

Practical approach: **treat SPC as a replaceable element** in your flood plan. Seal perimeters with silicone to slow water ingress. Keep spare planks from the same production batch. If major flooding happens, remove, dry the subfloor completely, reinstall with new material.

### Salt air - mostly fine actually

The PVC-limestone core doesn't corrode from salt exposure like metals or wood do. Click mechanism is the same material so same resistance. Repeated salt crystallization in the joints could cause some micro-abrasion over time but nothing dramatic.

Main thing: regular damp mopping with pH-neutral cleaner to remove salt deposits that dull the surface. Moisture-absorbing mats (not rubber-backed) at all entries. Avoid aggressive cleaning chemicals. No SPC product is marketed as "marine grade" but the material is inherently salt-tolerant.

## Buying SPC from Chinese factories

### Where SPC comes from

China makes **over 95% of the world's SPC flooring**. Four main production areas:

**Jiangsu Province** (Changzhou, Taizhou, Zhangjiagang) - the quality and scale leader. Biggest manufacturers here including Taizhou Huali (390,000 m² facility, 1,500 containers/month), Yihua (21,600+ containers/year). Major Western brands OEM here. Near Shanghai and Ningbo ports so logistics are easy. **This is where you want to source from ideally.**

**Zhejiang Province** (Haining, Jiaxing) - premium and design-focused. Advanced printing and EIR textures. Kingdom Plastics has been here since 1992, 45 million sqm annually, exports to 50+ countries. EPD certified through 2029.

**Shandong Province** (Chiping, Qingdao, Linyi) - over 60% of suppliers by count. Cheap limestone and PVC supply locally. Quality varies wildly. Some great factories next to a lot of sketchy operations and trading companies pretending to be factories. Lowest prices but highest risk.

**Guangdong Province** (Foshan, Guangzhou) - more of a trade and export hub. Close to Canton Fair. Good logistics. A lot of the "manufacturers" here actually source from Jiangsu or Shandong.

For this project? **Jiangsu or Zhejiang first**. Shandong if you're willing to vet harder.

### The three suppliers I looked at

This is a trading company or project facilitator. Working with them adds markup and reduces your control over product specs and quality verification. If you use them anyway, demand to know which specific factory produces your order and verify that factory independently.

**Prima Flooring** - appears to be a brand name used by Shandong Caishi Wood Industry Co., Ltd (listed on Made-in-China). Established 2014 in Shandong, 5 million sqm/year capacity, 70% exported. Their SPC range is 3.5-8.5mm thickness with 0.2-0.6mm wear layers. Claims ISO 9001, SGS testing, CE marking. At 10 years old it's relatively young, and Shandong province means cheaper but riskier production. Mid-range pricing. Worth verifying factory location independently and confirming "Prima" is actually who you're dealing with since it's a pretty common brand name.

**Bolande Flooring** (Foshan Bolande Industrial Company Limited) - confirmed Alibaba presence at bolande.en.alibaba.com. Based in Foshan, Guangdong. Lists SPC flooring tiles and WPC decking. Being in Foshan is interesting because Foshan is mainly a trade hub... lots of Foshan "manufacturers" actually source from Jiangsu or Shandong factories. Manufacturers with way stronger track records that I'd consider over those three:

- **Taizhou Huali** - since 2002, 390,000 m² facility, even has a US factory in Georgia

### How to tell if it's a real factory or a trader

Almost every Chinese supplier says they're a factory. Here's how you actually check:

**Business Scope on Chinese license** - a real factory must include manufacturing terms like 生产 (production), 加工 (processing), or 制造 (manufacturing). Trading companies won't have these.

Other tells:
- Real factories issue **17% VAT invoices** (needed for export tax drawback)
- Higher, less flexible MOQs
- Specialized product catalog (not tiles AND shoes AND electronics)
- Physical address in an industrial zone, not an office building in Shanghai

Verification tools: **Qichacha (企查查)** for checking Chinese company registration. **ImportYeti** for cross-referencing export shipping history. A third-party factory audit through SGS, Bureau Veritas, or TUV Rheinland runs $600-2,000 and gives definitive confirmation.

## Questions to grill your supplier with

### Raw materials - the real test

Ask these and watch how they respond. Hesitation = red flag:

- Exact percentage of virgin vs recycled PVC in the core?
- Calcium carbonate source, grade, mesh size? (target: 325+ mesh heavy calcium powder)
- What plasticizer - DOP, DOTP, or DINP? (DOTP preferred. DOP is a restricted phthalate under EU REACH)
- Stabilizer system - calcium-zinc or lead-based? (Lead is prohibited for export to most markets and is a genuine health hazard)
- UV coating brand, number of coats, gloss level?
- Actual wear layer thickness with micrometer measurements?

**The stabilizer question is particularly telling.** Quality factories immediately say calcium-zinc and cite export compliance. Factories that hesitate or change the subject might be using cheaper lead-based stabilizers. walk away.

### Production and sample verification

Ask about monthly capacity in sqm, brand of extrusion lines, lead time (typical 25-35 days).

Super important: **request production-line samples, NOT showroom samples**. Tell them you want pieces pulled from the current run, ideally with video evidence. Order at least 3-5 pieces to test click-lock fitment between planks. Ask for cross-section samples to inspect core color. Request both A-grade and B-grade pieces so you can see the quality gap.

For click systems: "Are you licensed for Unilin, Valinge, or I4F? Show me the license certificate and confirm L2C labels on every carton." If they claim licensing, verify directly with the licensor.

### Payment and inspection

Standard terms: **30% T/T deposit upon order confirmation, 70% balance against Bill of Lading copy**. Never ever pay 100% upfront. For first orders, Letter of Credit at sight is the strongest buyer protection.

[Alibaba Trade Assurance](/alibaba-sourcing) provides escrow protection but you need extremely detailed specs in the order contract. Vague stuff like "high quality" is unenforceable.

**Pre-shipment inspection is the single most important thing you can spend money on.** Hire SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, or QIMA (roughly $250-400 per man-day) to do random sampling when goods are 100% produced and at least 80% packed. They check dimensions (±0.1mm thickness tolerance), color consistency, click function, surface defects, core uniformity, packaging, L2C labels...

Skipping a $300 inspection to save money routinely costs buyers $10,000+ in defective goods. just don't skip it.

## Installation in Camotes - exceeds standard procedures

### Floating installation (yes, floating)

**Click-lock floating installation is the way to go** for this project. Reasons:

- Floor expands and contracts freely with temperature changes
- Easier to replace flood-damaged planks
- Vapor barrier goes underneath
- No adhesive failure risks in sustained high humidity

The only scenario where glue-down makes sense: rooms with intense direct sunlight through large windows on upper floors where flood risk is minimal. Pressure-sensitive adhesive rated for 95%+ RH can prevent thermal buckling. But for most rooms? float it.

**Expansion gaps: 10-12mm minimum** around all perimeters, columns, fixed objects. NOT the standard 6-8mm. That 10-meter floor run can expand 5mm in direct sun, remember?

**Transition strips every 6-8 meters** of continuous run. Standard temperate recommendation is 8-10 meters but tropical heat means more movement.

All baseboards and trim must be **wall-mounted, never floor-mounted**. The floor has to float freely underneath.

### Concrete subfloor prep (don't skip this)

Philippine concrete quality means you need to pay extra attention here. This is literally where the Malaysian failure case went wrong.

Slab must be **fully cured 60-90 days minimum**. In tropical humidity curing takes longer as moisture escapes more slowly. Test moisture with ASTM F2170 RH probes (target ≤85% RH) or ASTM F1869 calcium chloride test (target ≤5 lbs/1000 sq ft/24 hrs). Check pH (must be 7-9).

Flatness: 3-meter straightedge, **within 4.7mm per 3 meters**. Use Portland cement-based self-leveling compound (NOT gypsum, which is vulnerable to moisture) for any deviations.

Install a **10-mil polyethylene vapor barrier** over the entire concrete surface. 200-300mm overlaps, sealed with moisture-resistant tape. Standard spec is 6-mil but for coastal Philippines go thicker for better puncture resistance during installation.

Clean the subfloor thoroughly before barrier installation. Any organic debris left under the SPC becomes potential mold food in the trapped moisture environment. Don't be lazy about this step.

### Use marine-grade transition strips

At 150 meters from the sea, standard aluminum and brass will corrode. Period.

Use **316 stainless steel** (marine-grade) or **high-quality PVC** for all T-moldings, reducers, and end caps. Regular steel and brass? no.

316 stainless is the most expensive option but it's the only metal that reliably handles sustained salt air.

### Acclimatization

**48 hours minimum** with boxes opened and planks spread out in the room where they'll be installed. Since the villa probably won't have AC running yet, you're acclimatizing to actual ambient temperature (28-35°C). This means the planks are near their expanded state during installation, which actually helps reduce post-installation buckling from heat. Silver lining.

## Cost breakdown: China factory to Camotes Island floor

### ACFTA means zero customs duty

SPC flooring is classified under **HS 3918.10** (floor coverings of polymers of vinyl chloride). There was a July 2025 Philippine Tariff Commission Advance Ruling that specifically confirmed this classification for SPC planks with UV layer, wear layer, SPC core, decorative film, and IXPE foam.

Under the **ASEAN-China Free Trade Agreement (ACFTA)**, customs duty is **0%**. Zero. Compared to 10-15% under the standard MFN rate.

BUT you need a **Certificate of Origin Form E** from the Chinese supplier. **Always demand Form E.** Forgetting this adds 10-15% to your landed cost for literally no reason.

Remaining import costs: 12% VAT on CIF value, customs brokerage (5,000-15,000 pesos per container), arrastre and wharfage at Cebu port (3,000-8,000 pesos), marine insurance (0.5-1% of CIF value).

### What a 20ft container actually costs landed

A 20ft container holds about 2,200-2,500 sqm of SPC (weight is often the limiting factor because SPC is so dense). Ocean freight from Shanghai, Ningbo, or Guangzhou to Cebu port: $1,000-1,500, transit time 10-18 days.

For mid-range SPC at $6.00/sqm FOB:

|---|---|---|
| FOB China | $6.00 | ₱342 |
| Ocean freight | $0.52 | ₱30 |
| Marine insurance | $0.05 | ₱3 |
| **CIF Cebu** | **$6.57** | **₱374** |
| Customs duty (ACFTA 0%) | $0.00 | ₱0 |

Budget SPC at $3.50 FOB lands at roughly ₱299/sqm. Premium at $10 FOB comes to about ₱695/sqm.

### vs Local retail... yeah big difference

Wilcon Depot sells P.Tech SPC at **1,207-1,441 pesos/sqm** for mid-range to EIR-textured. Direct import at ₱440/sqm = **63% savings**.

Even compared to direct suppliers on Carousell and Facebook Marketplace (₱723-841/sqm for budget SPC), direct import saves 15-40%.

The savings justify the hassle of international sourcing pretty easily.

Installation labor in Cebu runs 150-350 pesos/sqm for SPC click-lock. Way cheaper than tile because no adhesive, grout, or wet work.

**Add 10-20% labor premium for Camotes** though. Fewer skilled workers available locally, mainland Cebu workers need transport and accommodation.

Budget another 100-300 pesos/sqm for subfloor prep (leveling compound, vapor barrier) and 50-100/sqm for trims and transitions.

A full container of 2,200-2,500 sqm at mid-range is roughly **$17,000-18,000 total** including all shipping, duties, and local logistics. That's enough for an entire villa with plenty of spare planks for repairs.
